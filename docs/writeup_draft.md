## 4. Baseline Model: Markov Chain (n-gram)

To evaluate the effectiveness of deep learning sequence models for music generation, we implemented a Markov Chain (n-gram) baseline. Using the same event-based token representation (356 tokens covering Note On, Note Off, and Time Shift), the Markov model computes next-token probabilities based on the strict empirical frequency of preceding contexts. To address sparsity, the model backs off to shorter context lengths and mixes with a smoothed unigram distribution (90/10 weight).

We trained models with contexts of size $n=2$ (order-2) and $n=3$ (order-3) on 3.24 million tokens of classical piano MIDI data. The order-2 model achieved a test accuracy of 19.7% and a test perplexity of 51.87. In contrast, increasing the context to $n=3$ degraded performance significantly (test accuracy: 17.3%, perplexity: 104.73), confirming that larger exact-match n-gram models suffer heavily from data sparsity in polyphonic music. In contrast, the LSTM effectively generalized over long sequences, achieving a superior test perplexity of 10.76.

## 5. Listening Survey (Music Turing Test)

Because mathematical loss and perplexity do not always correlate perfectly with perceived musicality, we conducted a blind "Music Turing Test". 

**Methodology:**
We created a Google Form survey presenting participants with 6 short (15-second) audio clips in a randomized, blinded order:
* 2 excerpts of real human-performed music (from the MAESTRO test set)
* 2 clips generated by the Markov baseline
* 2 clips generated by our trained LSTM

For each clip, participants were asked to guess if the music was "Human or Machine" and rate its musicality on a scale from 1 (Random noise) to 5 (Highly musical/coherent).
